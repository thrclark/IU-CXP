# The Role of Design Artifacts in the Campus Services Discovery Tool

## Purpose

This statement explains why our team is committing to a structured set of design artifacts throughout the design and development of the campus services discovery tool, and what each artifact contributes to the outcome. The project's core challenge is not a lack of functionality to build — IU already exposes hundreds of services, from Canvas and the Student Center to niche administrative tools like the Grants Management Toolkit or eDossier System — but a discovery problem: students, faculty, staff, parents, and guests across every IU campus need to find the right service quickly, without needing to already know its name. Design artifacts are how we make that problem tractable before a single line of production code depends on the wrong assumption, and how we keep the team, our stakeholders, and future maintainers aligned on what we're building and why.

## Discovery & Research Artifacts

Personas, journey maps, and information architecture work exist to make sure we are solving the right problem for the right people before we design any screen. IU's user base is unusually heterogeneous for a single tool: a first-year student looking for the bus tracker has almost nothing in common with a faculty member searching for the Faculty Center, a parent trying to set up authorized access, or a staff member navigating HRMS. Personas let us name and design against these distinct groups deliberately, rather than defaulting to a single "typical user" that quietly favors whichever group is loudest in the room. Journey maps take that further by tracing what a person is actually trying to accomplish — enrolling in a class, resolving a bursar bill, requesting a transcript — across every step and system involved, which is where we find the friction that a features list alone would never surface. Information architecture and site maps then translate the sheer breadth of services (well over a hundred, spanning academic, financial, administrative, and campus-life categories) into a navigable structure. Given the scale of the service catalog, IA work is arguably the single highest-leverage artifact on this project: get the categorization and findability model wrong, and no amount of visual polish in later stages will fix it.

## Structural & Interaction Artifacts

Wireframes and user/task flows let us test structure and logic cheaply, before visual design or engineering effort is sunk into a layout that doesn't work. Because this tool is fundamentally a search-and-browse experience layered over a large, varied catalog, the hard problems are structural: how search results are organized, how categories and filters behave, how a user gets from "I don't know what this is called" to "here is the service I need" in the fewest steps. Wireframes let the team and stakeholders react to that structure directly, without visual styling distracting from or masking a layout problem. Task flows do the same for logic — mapping out branches like what happens when a search returns zero results, when a service requires authentication the user doesn't have, or when a service applies to only one campus — so those edge cases are designed on paper rather than discovered mid-build.

## Visual & High-Fidelity Artifacts

UI mockups and interactive prototypes are where the design becomes concrete enough to evaluate and test with real people. High-fidelity mockups establish a consistent visual language — typography, color, iconography — across what will otherwise be a very long, very heterogeneous list of services, so the tool reads as one coherent product rather than a directory scraped together from disparate systems. Interactive prototypes let us put that design in front of students, staff, and faculty and watch them actually try to find something, which is the only reliable way to validate that the IA and flow decisions made earlier actually hold up under real use. Prototyping in a tool like Figma also lets us gather that feedback and iterate before any engineering time is spent, which matters on a project with this much surface area to get right.

## System & Technical Handoff Artifacts

Design systems, style guides, and handoff specifications are what let the design decisions made above actually survive contact with development, and survive it consistently across what will likely be a long-lived, frequently updated tool. A design system of reusable components (buttons, inputs, modals, cards for service listings) means every new service category or feature added after launch inherits the same visual and interaction language automatically, instead of drifting further from the original design with each update. Handoff specifications — padding, interactive states, and explicit WCAG accessibility requirements — remove ambiguity from the developer's side of the process and are especially important here: as a university system serving the public and current students, faculty, and staff, this tool has both a practical and a legal obligation to be usable by people relying on assistive technology, and that requirement is far cheaper to build in from redlines than to retrofit after launch.

## Summary

Taken together, these artifacts move the project through a deliberate sequence: understand who we're designing for and what they're trying to do, structure the experience around that understanding, make it visually and interactively concrete enough to test, and then hand it off in a form that development can build and extend consistently. Skipping a stage doesn't save time so much as move the cost downstream — a missing persona shows up later as a mockup that serves the wrong user, a missing wireframe shows up later as a visual redesign of a broken structure, and a missing handoff spec shows up later as an accessibility gap found after launch. For a tool with this much breadth and this diverse an audience, the artifacts are the mechanism by which the team stays coordinated and the end product stays coherent.
